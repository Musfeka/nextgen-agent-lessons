Context Engineering

How do we give an agent the right context — and why is "more" the wrong answer?

Time 15 minutes You will be able to rewrite a weak instruction using the context hierarchy Prerequisites Unit 2

This is the load-bearing unit of the course. A Skill is, mechanically, a way of delivering the right context at the right moment. If this unit lands, Units 5 and 6 are mostly formatting. If it does not, your team will write long, well-meaning Skills that make the agent worse.

3.1 From prompt engineering to context engineering

You have probably heard "prompt engineering." The field moved on, and the newer term is more useful because it is more honest about what you are actually managing.

Context engineering refers to the set of strategies for curating and maintaining the optimal set of tokens (information) during LLM inference, including all the other information that may land there outside of the prompts.

Anthropic, Effective context engineering for AI agents
Context engineering

Managing an agent's entire information state, turn after turn, as it loops. Ongoing, cumulative, and mostly about what to leave out.

Prompt engineering

Writing one good instruction, once. Still useful — but it is a subset. It ends when you press enter.

The context window is everything the model can see at one moment: your instruction, the documents, the conversation so far, tool results, and every Skill that has been loaded. Not just what you typed.

3.2 Why "give it everything" is a losing strategy

The instinct of every intelligent beginner is to paste in more. It is wrong, and there is a specific mechanism.

Context rot

"As the number of tokens in the context window increases, the model's ability to accurately recall information from that context decreases."

Adding information you do not need makes the information you do need harder to retrieve. Not neutral. Actively harmful.

The cause is architectural. Transformers relate every token to every other token, so n tokens create roughly n² pairwise relationships. Stretch that far enough and attention gets thin. Anthropic's framing is that models have an attention budget that depletes with every token added — much like human working memory.

So the governing principle for everything you write from here on:

Write this on the wall

"Find the smallest set of high-signal tokens that maximize the likelihood of some desired outcome."

Not the most context. The smallest set of the highest-signal context.

3.3 Context, knowledge, memory — three different things

Your team will use these interchangeably. Stop that early; it causes real design errors.

What it isLives whereFailure looks like
Knowledge What the model learned in training — English, software concepts, how a test case works. In the model. Free. Always there. Out of date, or generic where you needed specific.
Context What is in front of it this turn — your request, the document, tool results. The context window. Finite and costly. Missing, stale, or so bloated the signal drowns.
Memory What deliberately persists across turns or sessions — notes, files, saved state. Outside the window, retrieved on demand. Never written, or written and never read back.

The practical rule that falls out of this table, and the one that most improves people's Skills: never spend context on knowledge. If the model already knows what a user story is, explaining it is pure cost. Spend context only on what is yours.

3.4 The context hierarchy

When you assemble context — in a prompt now, in a Skill later — assemble it in this order. It is the same order you would brief a competent contractor.

  1. Goal
    What outcome are we producing? One sentence.
  2. Role
    Whose standards apply — a Senior BA? A compliance reviewer?
  3. Rules
    The non-negotiables. What must always or never happen.
  4. Knowledge
    Only what is specific to you: your template, your domain terms, your client's quirks.
  5. Inputs
    The actual material to work on, this time.
  6. Constraints
    Length, format, tone, scope, what to leave alone.
  7. Output
    The exact shape of the deliverable.

Steps 1–4 and 6–7 barely change between runs. Only step 5 changes. That stable part is your Skill — which is the entire insight of Unit 5, arrived at from the other direction.

3.5 The right altitude

There are two ways to write instructions badly, and they are opposites.

Too low — brittle

Exhaustive if-this-then-that logic covering every case you imagined. Breaks the moment reality presents case 47. Endless to maintain.

If the requirement mentions
payment, use template A.
If it mentions login, use
template B. If it mentions
both, use template A but…
Too high — vague

Sounds wise, gives no signal. The model has to guess what you meant, and will guess differently each time.

Analyse the requirement
thoroughly and produce a
high-quality, professional
output that meets our
usual standards.

The target is what Anthropic calls the Goldilocks zone: "specific enough to guide behavior effectively, yet flexible enough to provide the model with strong heuristics."

Right altitude
For each requirement, identify
the actor, the trigger, the
expected outcome and the
failure behaviour.

Flag any of the four that the
requirement does not state.
Never infer a failure
behaviour that is not written
down — ask instead.

Specific about what to look for and what is forbidden. Silent about how to look. That is the balance.

Why it works

It gives the model four concrete hooks, one hard rule with a stated escape hatch, and trusts it with everything else.

It also survives contact with a requirement type you did not anticipate — the brittle version does not.

3.6 Garbage context → garbage reasoning → garbage outcome

Worth saying plainly to a professional audience: when an agent produces a bad output, the fault is usually upstream. Before rewriting instructions, ask in order —

  • Was the necessary information present? (Perception.)
  • Was it findable, or buried in 40 pages of irrelevance? (Context rot.)
  • Was it current, or a stale copy of the spec?
  • Did it contradict something else in the window?

That last one is the quiet killer. Two versions of a requirement in one context does not average them out; it makes behaviour unpredictable.

The same failure, in six jobs
BA
Old and new BRD both in context. The agent cites the superseded acceptance criteria.
SQA
Whole 200-page test suite pasted in "for reference". It misses the two relevant existing cases.
UX
Research quotes given with no product context. The persona is plausible and useless.
Dev
Reviewing a diff with no sight of the interface it implements. Style feedback only.
PM
Status from three sources that disagree. The report confidently picks one.
BD
RFP given without the capability list. It promises something you cannot deliver.

Activity: repair a bad instruction

Here is a real-shaped, genuinely poor instruction. It is the kind your team will write in week one.

Look at the attached document and give me some test cases.
Make them good and cover everything important. Use your
judgement. Format it nicely.

Rewrite it using the seven-level hierarchy. Do not make it longer for the sake of it — make it higher-signal. A strong answer here is often shorter than you expect.

The point of the exercise

Read your rewrite back and notice how much of it you would say identically next week, for a different document. Highlight that part mentally. In Unit 5 you will learn that you just wrote a Skill without being told what one was.
