Evaluation, Validation and Human-in-the-Loop

How do we know the agent actually did the job correctly?

Time 16 minutes You will be able to diagnose why a Skill failed, and place the human gate correctly Prerequisites Unit 7

This is the unit that makes the rest professionally defensible. Any team can generate output. The teams that keep using agents after month three are the ones who built a way to tell good output from confident output.

The operating principle for your whole team

AI proposes. Human validates. The system improves.

Not "give the AI instructions and trust the result." Every rollout that skips the middle term ends the same way: one bad output reaches a client, and the organisation stops trusting the technology instead of fixing the loop.

8.1 Why agent outputs fail

Seven causes. The diagnostic value is that each has a different fix — which is why "the AI got it wrong" is a useless bug report.

The uncomfortable one

In our experience the last row is the most common in BA and SQA work, and the hardest to accept. If the agent produced a defensible reading of an ambiguous requirement, it found a real defect in your requirement. That is a win reported as a failure.

8.2 Six dimensions of Skill evaluation

"Is it good?" is unanswerable. Split it:

Accuracy

Is what it says true? Check against the source, not against plausibility.

Completeness

Did it cover everything in scope? Absences are invisible — look for them deliberately.

Consistency

Same input, same shape of answer? Run it three times before believing once.

Relevance

Did it answer the actual question, or an adjacent easier one?

Compliance

Does it obey your standards, template, regulatory constraints?

Explainability

Can it say why? An answer you cannot interrogate cannot be signed off.

Consistency is the one teams skip and then get burned by. A Skill that is excellent twice and wrong the third time is worse than one that is mediocre every time, because it teaches people to stop checking.

8.3 Deterministic checks versus judgement checks

Some of these can be checked by a rule. Some need reasoning. Knowing which is which is what makes evaluation affordable.

Deterministic — cheap, reliable, do these first
  • Every acceptance criterion has ≥1 test case
  • The output table has the required columns
  • No field is left as a placeholder
  • The word count is within bounds
  • Every claim carries a source reference
Judgement — expensive, necessary
  • Is this test scenario actually meaningful?
  • Is the risk assessment reasonable?
  • Does this critique reflect real usability harm?
  • Is this the right interpretation of intent?
The move that upgrades a Skill fastest

Find one judgement check and convert it into a deterministic one. "Is the coverage good?" is judgement. "Every acceptance criterion appears in at least one row of the output table" is a rule — and it can be written into the Skill as a self-check step the agent performs before finishing.

This is the feedback-loop pattern from Unit 6, now doing evaluation work. It is the single most valuable thing a non-technical author can add to a Skill.

8.4 Where the human goes

Four different interventions, routinely conflated. They have different costs and belong in different places.

InterventionQuestionPut it where
ReviewIs this right?Everywhere, initially. Relax as trust is earned.
ApproveMay this proceed?Before any write action, and before anything leaves the building.
OverrideI disagree — do it my way.Available always. If it is being used constantly, the Skill is wrong.
CorrectHere is what it should have been.Captured and fed back into the Skill. This is where improvement comes from.

Placing the gate

Three rules that will serve your team well:

  1. Gate on irreversibility, not importance. Anything that cannot be undone — sent, published, committed, deleted — gets a human. A draft nobody sees does not.
  2. Gate on the boundary of your control. The moment output leaves your team, a named person owns it.
  3. Gate early rather than late. Approving scope before 60 test cases are generated costs a minute. Reviewing 60 cases costs an afternoon — so nobody does it properly.

Rule 3 is the one people get wrong. Notice that step 7 of the Unit 2 test plan put the human at scope approval, not at case review. That was deliberate.

8.5 The improvement loop

Execute→ Evaluate→ Feedback→ Retry→ Improve the Skill↺

The highlighted step is where teams break the loop. People notice a bad output, fix it by hand, and move on. The correction never reaches the Skill, so it recurs forever.

Make it cheap and it will happen: keep a running note per Skill — date, what it got wrong, what it should have done. When there are three entries, revise. That is the Claude A / Claude B loop from Unit 6, with your observations as the input.

Activity: diagnose a failure

A BA installs a requirement-analysis Skill. Here is the input and the output.

REQUIREMENT (from the client's BRD):
"The system should notify relevant users when an order status changes."

SKILL OUTPUT:
Analysis complete. This is a functional requirement.
Actor: end users.
Trigger: order status change.
Expected outcome: notification sent via email within 5 minutes.
Priority: Medium.
No gaps identified. Ready for user story creation.

Read it again before answering. It looks entirely professional, which is the point.

Diagnosis

1"Notification sent via email within 5 minutes" appears in the output. What is that?

2"No gaps identified" is the most serious line in the output. Why?

3Which single change to the Skill would prevent most of this?

4Where should the human gate sit for this Skill?

Write the evaluation for your own Skill

Three scenarios, with expected behaviour stated. This is the artefact from Unit 6, step 2 — write it now for the Skill you built.

The one everyone forgets

The third rubric item: a scenario testing what the Skill should refuse to do. Almost nobody writes one. It is the scenario that catches the failure you just diagnosed above, and it is the difference between a Skill that is helpful and a Skill that is trustworthy.
